The Transatlantic Slave Trade transported between ten and twelve million enslaved
Africans across the Atlantic, most from West Africa — Senegambia, Sierra Leone, the
Bight of Benin, the Bight of Biafra, the Gold Coast, the Windward Coast. Places such as
Bunce Island functioned both as a military training base and a major departure point,
its entryways becoming known as gateways of hell. Thousands of Africans — men, women,
children — passed through it into a future built on suffering, neglect, violence, and
dehumanization.

The Middle Passage was gruelling: disease, claustrophobia, thirst, psychological
torment. Fifteen to twenty percent of Africans on these voyages died before reaching
the Americas. But what I'd never really thought about until engaging with the readings
was the impact on those who never boarded a ship at all — the slave trade didn't only
wreck the lives of the enslaved, it wrecked entire communities across West Africa.

Reading Rosalind Shaw's _Memories of the Slave Trade_ opened a door in my mind. It
seems obvious in hindsight that West Africa was deeply affected by the slave trade, but
most of what I'd been taught were memories of those forced onto the ships, not of those
who remained. They lived through enormous instability — social, political, spiritual.
European traders intensified warfare and competition between communities, adding
pressure where there had been none. Enslaved men carried higher "market value," which
created gender imbalances and more unbalanced power dynamics; the resulting polygynous
marriages increased wealthy merchants' power while reducing the rights of wives who
were themselves enslaved within those marriages. I hadn't known that communities raided
each other, capturing rivals to sell to European traders — economic desperation and
pressure from the trade itself, prioritised over any country's own social and economic
development.

Witchcraft takes on deep significance in this context. Shaw's work on Sierra Leone
traces how memories of the slave trade continue to shape modern ideas about witchcraft,
spiritualism, and divination — and it makes a lot of sense against basic human
instincts: "why me," "why not someone else," "why." A way of making sense of
suffering, misfortune, injustice. Shaw quotes John Matthews, an agent for an English
slave-trading firm writing in the late eighteenth century: "If an alligator destroys
any body when washing or swimming, or a leopard commits depredations on their flocks or
poultry; if any person is taken suddenly ill, or dies suddenly, or is seized with any
disaster they are not accustomed to, it is immediately attributed to witchcraft: and it
rarely happens that some person or other is not pointed out by their conjurors, whom
they consult on these occasions, as the witch — and sold" [1961 (1788):130–132].

During the slave trade, people connected spiritual forces and violence — often
described as apocalyptic — to the world collapsing around them. Witchcraft explained
enslavement, the loss of kin, the collapse of community. Divination brought forward
tales of spirits, ancestors, and mystical beings that grew more intense through this
period; powers once understood as capable of both good and harm came to be seen as more
dangerous, more purely evil. Divination became less about the illness itself and more
about who or what was responsible for the suffering.

Learning more about _Bondye_, the _lwa_, and the wider category of spirits and the
divine across these traditions, I've started noticing more resemblance than
difference between them — though plenty would argue otherwise, often out of pride.
Across these systems there's a shared desire to make meaning out of the mundane, to
find freedom from what's invisible or oppressive, to connect to something greater than
oneself — a higher power to pray to, dance to, feel. A sense of hope or justice that
wrongdoing will be answered, and that suffering has an explanation. In the end, these
traditions all circle the same questions: the what, the where, the who, and — most of
all — the why.
