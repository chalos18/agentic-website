This is a little detour through the history of mathematics — specifically, the
overlap between mining, mathematics, and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's experiments with
drainage machinery in the 1600s. I wanted to answer one question: did Leibniz use his
own calculus in his attempts at mining engineering? And whichever way the answer went,
I wanted to understand why, using both his mathematical inventions and the technical
state of mining and geology at the time.

Leibniz was a German polymath from the 1600s — mathematician, philosopher, inventor,
historian — who, alongside Isaac Newton, is credited with independently developing
calculus in the late seventeenth century. Their approaches diverged in a telling way:
Newton, who'd already made major discoveries in physics, treated calculus through the
lens of motion — derivatives as fluxions, integrals as fluents — using mathematics to
describe how objects move. Leibniz concentrated instead on infinite series and sums,
visualising integrals as the sum of infinitely many rectangles under a curve.

In 1674 Leibniz began approaching mathematics with a metaphysical bent, and recognised
the relationship between integration and differentiation — the groundwork for a new
mathematical system. By late 1675 he had the foundations of both integral and
differential calculus: integration as the sum of infinitely many small quantities,
differentiation as the rate of change found through infinitely small differences — an
understanding that led directly to his formulation of the fundamental theorem of
calculus. He treated variables like _x_ and _y_ as taking on infinitely close
consequent values. He published the theory in 1684, introducing much of the notation
still in use today. The Leibniz Archive in Hannover holds around 50,000 pieces of his
writing, roughly 200,000 pages, some of which look strikingly close to modern calculus
notation.

Between 1680 and 1686, Leibniz made several trips to the Harz Mountains to develop
wind-powered drainage machines for the region's silver mines, which suffered from
severe flooding — water blocking access to some of the richest veins, to the point that
draining them cost more than the ore they'd yield. Dewatering needed pumps capable of
high-pressure extraction; Leibniz initially proposed a pump that could lift water from
over 1,000 feet down at a lower cost than existing methods. His first plan relied on
direct drainage, but by 1680 he'd revised it to recycle water into holding ponds for
reuse as waterpower — a design he called the "horizontal machine," combining wind and
water energy.

His lack of practical mining experience proved a major obstacle. In the seventeenth
century, groundwater behaviour in complex geological settings was barely understood —
Darcy's Law, describing groundwater flow through porous media, wouldn't exist for
another 176 years. Without that hydrological grounding, predicting risks like water
inrush or sand bursts from breached water-bearing formations was mostly guesswork. Harz
mining officials themselves recognised that success depended on "a combination of good
principles and long experience, of universal and local knowledge" — and Leibniz was
short on the latter, including something as basic as consistent wind for his horizontal
machine, and the practical depth of groundwater and pumping head he was working
against.

Leibniz developed differential calculus between 1674 and 1684, overlapping directly
with his time at the Harz mines. There's no direct evidence he applied calculus to the
pump designs themselves, but his approach to the mining problem — combining wind and
water forces while trying to eliminate unnecessary friction — clearly reflects the same
mathematical and mechanical instinct. He designed windmills, gearing mechanisms, and
suction pumps, all of which ultimately failed due to weak, inconsistent wind. And
despite believing them revolutionary, his horizontal windmills had already existed for
centuries elsewhere, in China and Persia as early as the thirteenth century.

From a modern vantage point, water drainage and hydrological systems depend heavily on
calculus. Differential calculus calculates flow rates and the power needed to lift water
from thousands of feet down while overcoming friction; integral calculus handles total
work, energy over time, and irregular inflows from geological conditions — fluid
dynamics as a field is dominated by differential equations, making calculus fundamental
to pump design and groundwater management. Integration also lets you compute volumes
where curvature is involved — the method of discs and cross-sections extends
two-dimensional integration into three dimensions, making it possible to estimate the
volume of an irregular ore body. Modern geological modelling still leans on calculus to
understand how ore deposits are spatially distributed. Leibniz's own notation — the
integral sign, the use of _d_ for differentials — underpins all of it.

Geology kept building on this across the centuries that followed: Darwin's geological
mapping in the 1800s, Gauss's study of Earth's magnetic field in 1838, magnetometers
detecting ore deposits by signal strength in the 1930s. By the 1970s, geologists were
using computers to analyse slope stability and other complex mining problems, and today
three-dimensional geological modelling software is standard for predicting and
analysing underground conditions — because understanding what's underground, at all,
depends on modelling, processing, and retrieving data, which needs a mathematical
foundation Leibniz's work in the 1600s helped lay down. Techniques like slope stability
analysis (the Limit Equilibrium Method, for instance) and computer-based geological
modelling all build on the calculus he helped establish.

So: Leibniz likely never used his own calculus while he was actually at the Harz mines
— his era simply lacked the geological and engineering data needed to make it useful
there. But his differential calculus turned out to be indispensable anyway, just
decades and centuries later, in geological modelling, civil engineering, energy
projects, and environmental management — fields where understanding groundwater flow
through complex hydrogeology now matters constantly, for active operations as much as
for mine closure and rehabilitation.
