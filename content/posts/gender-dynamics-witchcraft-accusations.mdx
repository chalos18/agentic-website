Throughout my readings on witchcraft accusations for ANTH266, I noticed an interesting
pattern. When most of us picture a witch today, the image is shaped largely by popular
culture and media — but it almost always shares one thing in common: it's a woman.
Historically, though, the term "witch" is gender-neutral, deriving from the Old English
_wicce_ (female) and _wicca_ (male). Realising that made me question where my own
assumption came from, that witchcraft was inherently female, and it sent me back to my
readings to understand the cultures that shaped these accusations. What became clear is
that social dynamics — especially around gender — played a central role in how
witchcraft was understood and prosecuted.

In England between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries, between seventy and ninety
percent of those accused of witchcraft were women (Lewsey 2023). But that pattern wasn't
global. To understand why women were so disproportionately targeted in England, you
have to look at the social and religious framework of the period: women were believed
to be spiritually and physically weaker than men, and therefore more vulnerable to
temptation and demonic influence. Because of that, women were thought more easily drawn
into pacts with Satan — a belief central to witchcraft accusations at the time, which
were deeply embedded in a religious worldview.

Power dynamics reinforced this. Accusing a woman was often socially safer and more
plausible than accusing a man, especially given prevailing stereotypes about female
weakness and moral instability, and given that men held legal and social authority over
nearly everything. Women became easy targets within a system already structured around
gendered roles — which explains why English witchcraft accusations skewed so heavily
toward women, and sets up a striking comparison with a place like Iceland, where the
pattern reverses almost entirely.

In Iceland, approximately ninety-three percent of those accused of witchcraft were men
(Irvine 2025) — a shocking contrast to England that starts to make sense once you look
at Icelandic cultural and social dynamics. Icelandic society was shaped by Vikings,
Norse gods, and folklore, all heavily male-centred. Within that culture, magic was
understood as a masculine skill, associated with knowledge and power — traits more
commonly attributed to men — so men were seen as more capable of practising magic and,
in turn, more likely to be suspected of it. It's striking that in both cultures men hold
the power, yet that power gets interpreted in opposite directions when it comes to who
gets accused.

Another factor I found interesting: part of what shielded Icelandic women from
accusation was their limited access to property and land ownership. Because Icelandic
women generally lacked independent financial or legal status, they were less attractive
targets for prosecution in the first place (Irvine 2025) — accusation, in this
particular system, tracked property almost as much as it tracked gender.

This research has been genuinely enlightening in showing how culture and social
structure shape patterns of accusation. Witchcraft trials were closely tied to gender
roles, power, and local belief systems — a sensitive and complex topic I'd been
curious about for a long time, and one this research has helped me actually understand,
rather than just assume.
