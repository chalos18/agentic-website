Engaging with Peter Marshall's work on Orkney witchcraft accusations made me
increasingly aware of how allegations of witchcraft sat inside deeper conflicts about
power, belief, and social order. Focusing on Orkney highlighted how these tensions
played out in small communities where social relations were intimate but fraught —
alongside Miranda Forsyth's work on balancing the "emic" and "etic" viewpoints in
policy and law. Witchcraft accusations were never just guided by fear; they were also
about regulating the boundaries of acceptable social behaviour, especially for women.

One of the most striking insights from my reading was the degree of misogyny
underpinning Orkney's witchcraft cases. The vast majority of accused witches were
women, and that can't be a coincidence. I'd already had casual conversations with
colleagues about witchcraft and gender before this, but reading Marshall made those
discussions sharper and more relevant — confirming that these accusations weren't
random, but rooted in the social policing of women.

What struck me was how many of the accused were healers or charmers — skills that made
them indispensable to their communities but also dangerously ambiguous. In Orkney, and
across much of Europe, the line between "healer," "witch," "wise woman," and
"maleficent practitioner" was never clear-cut; the work of healing could easily be
reinterpreted as harm. Marshall shows how women's knowledge of medicines, charms, and
the management of illness made them both valued and feared — a contradiction that
feels painfully familiar. That connects back to earlier readings on healing in Jamaica
and the practice of Balm, where the categories of healer, witch, diviner, and
spiritual practitioner blurred just as easily. Women's "wiseness" can be celebrated
when it suits social norms and punished when it challenges them — a pattern that
recurs across the Atlantic world.

I was also struck by how accusations emerged from ordinary human interaction:
neighbours reporting suspicions, friends and rivals reinterpreting past misfortunes,
authorities translating rumour into prosecution. Once someone was declared a witch,
other people's memories of past resentments and unresolved anxieties sharpened
retroactively — ordinary misfortune reinterpreted as a witch's work. I saw the same
dynamic later in readings on Haitian Vodou: the human need to find an answer, not just
accountability. Combined with women being seen as weaker and more easily influenced,
that made them easy targets. Women in Orkney were often socially marginal, lacking
economic resources, political power, and legal autonomy — and were characterised as
more susceptible to the Devil's influence. The accusations functioned, in effect, as a
way to regulate women's agency.

Witchcraft regulation was deeply political too — a way of stamping out practices
associated with "unreformed" Catholicism and older supernatural traditions. What
fascinated me was how ordinary people read disability or unexplained sickness as
interference by fairies or possession by an evil spirit, and how illness itself could
be understood as _transferable_ rather than curable — a "zero-sum" process, where if
one person's illness left them, it had to go somewhere else. That explains why clean
distinctions between charmer and witch, healer and harmer, were so hard to hold: healing
and harming were too closely connected in Orkney's understanding of sickness. The wise
woman who cured might also have cursed.

Orkney's witchcraft cases tie into a broader Atlantic pattern — Europe, Bahia, West
Africa — where witchcraft regulation became a way to police behaviour, discipline
communities, and reinforce social hierarchies. What stays with me most is that my
original curiosity about witch trials started with questions of gender, and those
questions kept coming back with every reading. Gender is rarely a background factor
here; it's a central force behind the accusations, and that's deepened how I read the
Atlantic world — and what patterns still hold today.
