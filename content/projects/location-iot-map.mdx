Final project for PROD385 (Special Topic: Gadgets and Gizmos): a location-aware IoT
system that turns digital presence into physical, mechanical feedback. A phone's
geofencing publishes MQTT messages over a HiveMQ Cloud broker when I enter or leave key
locations; two Raspberry Pis running Node-RED subscribe and react by lighting the
matching LED on a laser-engraved, Monopoly-style board of Christchurch. An LCD1602
shows an availability message alongside live weather pulled from an API, and a PIR
sensor refines "at home" into "home and actually present."

## Building the board

The map itself is a 30×30cm plywood panel, laser-engraved with Christchurch's streets
and raised on an 8cm wooden support frame to leave room underneath for wiring and
soldered joints. I was not the most woodworking-savvy person going into this, and the
Product Design department's lab technicians were a big part of why the engraving came
out as clean as it did, and why the frame's glue joints actually held under clamping.

Each location gets a pair of LED legs pushed through the board from below, soldered
onto female headers rather than left loose — enough to translate a breadboard prototype
into something that survives being handled as a physical object.

## Software: MQTT, Node-RED, and the LCD

Two Node-RED flows do the reacting. One subscribes to a `python/mqtt` topic, maps each
incoming location payload to a GPIO pin, and switches the matching LED on while forcing
every other one off, so only one location is ever lit at a time. The second subscribes
to `home/presence`, published by the PIR sensor's Raspberry Pi, lights the home LED, and
publishes an acknowledgment back so the sensor side can confirm the round trip actually
worked rather than assuming it did.

from the OpenWeather API — truncated to 16 characters per line and only rewritten when
the content actually changes, to avoid visible flicker.

## The climbing mechanism

The part I enjoyed most was the mechanical subsystem: a TT DC motor driven through an
L293D driver winds fishing line around a spool to raise and lower a LEGO climber against
a textured panel — a small physical flourish on top of an otherwise all-digital system.
The motor driver matters here beyond convenience: GPIO pins can't supply the current a
motor needs, and driving one directly from a Pi or Arduino pin risks the pin itself, so
the L293D handles both direction and PWM speed control between the microcontroller and
the motor.

Getting there meant picking up skills well outside my usual stack — soldering, wiring a
PIR sensor and I²C LCD, laser-engraving the board, and building the support frame —
again with a lot of help from the lab technicians, who were far better than me at both
woodworking and knowing why an LED wouldn't light.
