The opening scenes of Mattijs Van de Port's 2016 essay film _The Possibility of
Spirits_ struck me immediately, signalling the filmmaker's approach to the wider
theoretical debates about spirit possession. Van de Port begins at the Miracle of Saint
Roque, a site of ritual where water drips from the rock — holy water. He explains that
his father was a geologist who grew up learning that rocks "sweat," a simple
geological process. By introducing the film through a scientific lens, he invites
skeptical viewers to feel comfortable with the uncomfortable — while setting up a
contrast with the Candomblé people, who consider the same water sacred. The tension
between scientific and spiritual interpretation is set up in the film's first few
minutes, and it's key to how the film engages with questions of ontology and
contrasting worldviews.

It quickly becomes clear that this documentary isn't trying to prove or disprove the
existence of spirits, but to explore other ways of understanding. Van de Port positions
himself as someone stepping outside his comfort zone from the start — observing rather
than interrogating, letting what he sees challenge his own assumptions. That connects
to a much broader anthropological debate about how to represent spirit possession
without reducing it to psychology, folklore, or superstition.

The film's central challenge is really the viewer's own preconceptions. Different
religions and cultural backgrounds shape how people interpret "spirits," and for many
the concept gets demonised or dismissed outright. Growing up in Brazil, where African
spiritual traditions and Christianity often blend, I recognised how multiple
understandings of spirits can coexist. The film invites viewers to sit with other
ontologies, and through that, Van de Port contributes to a wider debate about belief,
rationality, and the coexistence of multiple worldviews — helping the viewer understand
not just the possibility of spirits, but the challenge of acknowledging them at all.

One of the film's strongest techniques is what I'd call "the showing of not showing."
Rather than filming spirit-possession events head-on, Van de Port uses selective
framing to keep us just outside the ritual, making us question and pay attention to
symbolic detail instead. I found this confusing at first, but came to understand it as
respecting what can't always be shown. We do see plenty of the preparation, though —
children cutting fruit, the washing and folding of white cloths, the handling of ritual
elements — and the symbolism built from that imagery is strong: the meticulously washed
and folded white cloth, later stained by the blood of animal sacrifice. Purity and
blood, held in the same frame, carrying the sense of offering at the ritual's centre.
Van de Port admits at points that he didn't fully engage with what was happening while
filming — that he focused on the animal sacrifice and missed the meaning of the songs
praising the Green Feather. That admission matters, because in the Western world many
viewers have never witnessed an animal sacrifice, and it's easy to fixate on the
unfamiliar rather than the ritual significance underneath it.

The kaleidoscope scene is another strong example — visual experimentation used to
express being inspired by Obaluaê, the orixá of healing, illness, and death, tying back
into the film's white-cloth symbolism. The film never shows possession directly, but it
does build a deep sense of the wider setting the act takes place in.

The film includes the voices of several Candomblé people and, in doing so, attempts to
privilege emic perspectives — we hear people describe their experiences, rituals, and
relationships with spirits. But as a native Portuguese speaker, I felt a real amount of
emotional tone and meaning was lost in translation. One man spoke about deforestation
weakening the presence of spirits, repeating certain phrases with strong emphasis that
the English subtitles flattened out. Translation is especially hard in a context like
Candomblé, where metaphor, rhythm, and intention carry as much meaning as the literal
words — and a good translator needs to capture emotion and cultural weight, not just
surface phrasing. That tension does conflict with the filmmaker's stated preference for
minimal narration, though, so while multiple voices are present in the film, their
emotional and cultural richness doesn't always reach non-Portuguese-speaking viewers.
The emic voice is present, but not always fully privileged — a perspective I hold in
part because I feel personally connected to the words each person spoke, and it felt
like a privilege to hear them explain something this complex in the language they're
most comfortable in.

The film's cultural and historical context feels incomplete, too. Van de Port briefly
mentions the history of African enslavement in Brazil and how the cultures brought by
enslaved people shaped Bahia's spiritual landscape, which is necessary and helps explain
why Candomblé is so strong there compared to other regions — but it could have gone
much further. We're not given a clear account of who the orixás are, what possession
means within Candomblé, or why particular rituals matter; roles within a _terreiro_, who
is eligible for possession, how often rituals happen, are all left out. As a viewer
unfamiliar with Candomblé, that made some moments — like the references to werewolves
near the start — appear and disappear without explanation, feeling more like
philosophical wandering than ethnographic content. The film also gestures at regional
variation (a man mentions rituals held in forests outside Bahia, in São Paulo) without
exploring it — I wished it had asked whether Candomblé practice varies by state, whether
spirits are tied to local landscape, whether someone from outside Bahia could be
possessed by a Bahian spirit.

The filmmaker's presence is made extremely clear throughout — to the point where it
often overshadows the Candomblé participants themselves. His personal reflections,
uncertainties, and philosophical questions sit at the centre of the film, which becomes
less about Candomblé spirituality and more about Van de Port's own journey toward
understanding it. That creates real tension: it weakens the film's ethnographic depth
by keeping emic voices secondary, but it's also clearly intentional — the hesitations,
the gaps, the "showing by not showing," the deliberate ambiguity, all reflect his
desire to make viewers feel his own discomfort approaching a spiritual world outside his
frame of reference. In that sense, his dominance becomes part of the film's argument
about the difficulty of witnessing spirit possession at all. Every scene feels chosen
because of what it meant to him personally — what he was thinking, feeling, or
questioning in the moment — so his role as filmmaker isn't simply observational, it's
deeply interpretive, almost a new genre blending philosophical reflection with
ethnographic imagery.

I remain critical of how much his voice overshadows the Candomblé people in the film,
but I can appreciate the reflexive aim behind it. Van de Port isn't pretending to offer
a neutral account — he's inviting the viewer to experience the possibility of spirits
through his own uncertainty, curiosity, and emotional response. In that way, his
presence becomes the central interpretive frame of the entire film.
