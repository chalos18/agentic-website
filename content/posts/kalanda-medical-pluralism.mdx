Before watching _Kalanda: The Knowledge of the Bush_, I never fully understood how
differently people develop ideas about disease etiologies. I tended to assume that
Western understandings of illness could address or cure anything anyone had. In
Western education, we are taught that diseases emerge from biological dysfunction and
that factors such as stress, anxiety, and depression can weaken the immune system. We
also learn that lifestyle choices — exercise, time outdoors, social connection — can
improve mental and physical health. These same principles are equally fundamental in
healing traditions across Africa. Recognizing the similarities made it easier for me to
connect to practices such as Jamaican Balm and those practiced in Donsoya.

The Western similarities to the practice of Balm in Jamaica are fascinating. Feminine
power is vital to the healing process, which resonates with the Western labour most
associated with women — nurses, caretakers. Balm healers function almost like
psychologists: they spend far more time with their patients than Western doctors
typically do, listening to experiences of "bad feelings," weakness, headaches, or a
sense of "heaviness" in the head. Many of their treatments focus on calming,
emotionally restorative activities — music, touch, prayer, singing — that resemble
therapeutic practices known to improve mental health. It was interesting to see how
similar our experiences are, just described with different words.

Mother Jones, for example, demonstrates an intuitive, holistic thinking that lets her
interpret a case from just a few subtle clues. Her ceremonies provide treatment and
emotional arousal — important components of therapy — but also community-building: the
patient becomes part of a shared ritual as they join the church and continue treatment.
This fits with how every culture develops institutionalized ways to resolve emotional
conflicts and satisfy psychological needs. Balm healing appears not only medical but
profoundly relational.

Prior to the film and readings, I would often wonder why some cultures prefer
traditional healers to conventional doctors. I now understand that confusion came from
not knowing the concept of _disease etiology_ — the culturally specific explanations
for where illness comes from. In Jamaica and many other places, conventional-medicine
explanations are only one part of a much wider etiological system: illnesses may be
attributed to ghosts, ancestor spirits, obeah, demons, fallen angels, or God's
chastisement. These explanations aren't irrational; they sit within a worldview that
recognizes spiritual, moral, and social dimensions of health. It's the Western
worldview that's narrower here, not theirs.

The coexistence of multiple healing systems is _medical pluralism_ — using more than
one medical tradition to treat illness. I realised I already practice it myself: when I
feel unwell, I might take conventional medication, drink herbal tea, and have family
pray for me. I'd simply never heard the term for it.

The _donso_ of Burkina Faso also operate within medical pluralism, blending
plant-based pharmacology, ritual protection, spiritual communication, and embodied
discipline. Entering _donsoya_ is described as entering a kind of army: the bush is
full of dangers — animals, spirits, genies — and each requires special knowledge.
Hunters tie amulets to their guns to prevent misfires, wash with medicinal plants for
protection, and use powders to guard against accidents. Rituals accompany the cutting
of plants because each plant has a genie, a spiritual master. One striking moment in
the film describes children washed with medicines believed to strengthen them so deeply
that Western medicine no longer works on them — it's become part of their bones, as one
of the _donso_ puts it. Watching that made me wonder what plants were actually being
used, how their properties were discovered, and what's happening physiologically and
psychologically alongside the ritual meaning.

Both _Kalanda_ and Jamaican Balm taught me that medical pluralism is the global norm.
People everywhere draw on multiple systems of knowledge to heal body, mind, and spirit
— and biomedical, spiritual, and emotional explanations of illness coexist because each
addresses a different dimension of human experience.
