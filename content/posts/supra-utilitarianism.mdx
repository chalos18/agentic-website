Most presentations look like this: a room, some slides, maybe a few bullet points.
Efficient, useful, gets the job done — utilitarian, in the sense of being practical and
functional. I'll come back to that. But first, mathematics.

To explain supra-utilitarianism, we first need utilitarianism: traditionally, the
greatest good for the greatest number. To give an example beyond the classic trolley
problem — if I, a utilitarian, were to bake a cake for myself, I'd bake a plain, simple
loaf: efficient, cheap, nourishing. Why waste time or ingredients decorating a cake if
simplicity satisfies the craving and minimises effort and cost? A supra-utilitarian
wouldn't bake for utility alone. The cake becomes a gesture — a symbol of care, wonder,
creativity — expanding happiness through expression rather than just meeting a need.
Where a utilitarian measures happiness gained per unit of effort, a supra-utilitarian
asks what happiness could even mean beyond that.

We can see the same shift in mathematics. It began as a tool — counting grain, dividing
land, predicting the stars — mathematics as a servant of trade, agriculture,
administration. Purely utilitarian. But then something interesting happened:
Mesopotamian mathematics stopped being only about counting grain or building canals.
Scribes started asking questions that went beyond practical need. As Assyriologist
Eleanor Robson (2008) puts it, mathematics in Mesopotamia became "the world's first
pure mathematics" — _pure_, meaning what we start to see in Pythagorean triples,
Babylonian triangle geometry, "cow's nose" figures, and geometric puzzles with no
direct application at all. That's supra-utilitarianism: a leap beyond usefulness, into
creativity and virtuosity. Jens Høyrup describes it as "mathematics which formally
looks as if it could serve in scribal practice, but whose substance goes beyond what
could ever be needed."

It's worth being precise here: this isn't about something being _anti_-useful, just
_more than_ useful. You can find the same move in art, where a sketch has no
application but is really an experiment in form and proportion — or in an elaborately
decorated cake, when a plain loaf would do just fine. Human knowledge often leaps
forward exactly when people indulge supra-utilitarian curiosity. Mathematics — and
human creativity generally — has never stayed confined to the realm of utility.

Which brings me back to the start, and to why this presentation went the way it did.
I could have used PowerPoint and a few nice pictures — minimised my time and effort,
the fully utilitarian choice. Instead I gave this talk inside Minecraft, a game plenty
of people already know and love, because it makes the whole thing more performative and
playful: not something I had to do, but something I wanted to try. It isn't necessarily
_useful_ for a presentation about the history of mathematics — but it's fun and
expressive, and that was the point. This entire presentation was, deliberately, itself
an act of supra-utilitarianism.

Apparently it landed: my peers scored presentations on a 1-5 scale, and this one came
out on top — People's Choice.
